Chemical Kinetics

Chemical kinetics is the study of how quickly a chemical reaction occurs and how different factors influence their rate of reaction. While thermodynamics determine whether a reaction is possible or not, kinetics explain how long these reactions actually take. The rate of reaction is defined as “the measure of the change in concentration of the reactants or the change in concentration of the products per unit time” (Chem Libretexts, n.d.). Understanding reaction rates is crucial as it allows scientists to predict how much time is needed for a useful amount of product to be formed from a reaction, this is particularly important in food production and the manufacturing of pharmaceuticals. Understanding reaction rates can also help scientists optimise production ensuring a good product yield within a reasonable time frame.

The main factors which influence the rate of a reaction are the concentration of the reactants, the presence of a catalyst, the temperature and the pressure (if the reactants are gases). For example, if the temperature is increased, there molecules will have more kinetic energy which will lead to an increased frequency of collisions and therefore more successful molecular collisions causing the reaction to occur more quickly. Similarly, increased pressure will force the gas particles closer together, decreasing the distance between them, causing a greater number of successful collisions which would lead to an increased reaction rate.

Rate Laws and Reaction Order

Rate laws are mathematical equations that demonstrate how “the rate of a chemical reaction depends on reactant concentration” (Khan Academy, 2014). Generally, the rate law is written in the form

Rate= k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ

where k is a rate constant and m and n are exponents which are called reaction orders and convey how the concentration of each separate reactant affects the reaction rate. According to Atkins and de Paula (2006), the magnitude of rate constant, k, is dependent on the temperature and is not influenced by the concentration of the reactants. The values of the exponents in a rate law (reaction orders) cannot be predicted from a balanced equation: they must be determined experimentally as they depend on the reaction mechanism.

Reaction orders are important as they can classify reactions and predict how concentrations will vary and how much time is needed for the reaction to reach completion or equilibrium. Reactions in which the concentration of reactants is independent of the reaction rate are called zero order reactions. In first order reactions, the reaction rate is directly proportional to the concentrations of one reactant. Second order reactions typically involve either two molecules of the same substance or two molecules of two different substances reacting together. In such reactions the reaction rate is proportional to the product of the reactant concentrations. (Reid, n.d.)

In this project, the simulations assume first order kinetics for each step of the reaction. First order kinetics are frequently used in computational modelling as it simplifies the mathematical aspect, making it easier to understand, visualise and interpret how a system will behave.

Reversible Reactions and Dynamic Equilibrium

Many chemical reactions do not go to completion. Instead they occur as reversible reactions in which the product molecules can decompose and reform the original reactants. These reactions are indicated by a double arrow (⇌). At the beginning of the reaction, the forward reaction is dominant as there are more reactant particles present and concentration is high, which increases the frequency and success rate of collisions. As the reaction progresses, the number of product molecules increases and reactant concentration decreases causing the forward rate to become slower, allowing the reverse reaction rate to gradually increase.

Eventually, the forward reaction rate becomes equal to the reverse reaction rate, as shown in Figure 1, resulting in the reaction reaching a state called dynamic equilibrium. As shown in Figure 2, at this point, the concentration levels off and all species remain constant; however they are not equal to each other. Although the reaction has reached equilibrium, reactions are still occurring at molecular level as reversible reactions never proceed to completion, resulting in no net change in concentration (Revision Dojo 2025). In order for a reversible reaction to reach dynamic equilibrium, it must be in a closed system, where no reactants or products can leave or enter the reaction environment. This ensures that the changes in concentration are solely caused by the reaction.

Le Chatelier's principle states that “If a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in concentration, pressure, or temperature, the system will shift its equilibrium position so as to counter the effect of the disturbance” (Brown et al., 2012, p. 631). The position of equilibrium can vary depending on different conditions such as concentration, pressure and temperature. If equilibrium favors the products, the mixture will contain a greater proportion of products. In contrast, if it favours reactants, the opposite occurs and the mixture will contain more unreacted molecules. For example increasing the concentration of a reactant will cause the equilibrium to shift to the right, resulting in an increased yield.

Mathematical Modelling of Reaction Systems

Mathematical modelling is often used in Chemistry to demonstrate how changes occur in chemical systems over a period of time using equations rather than physical experiments. These models describe how quantities vary as the reaction progresses and allows scientists to predict reaction behaviours, efficiently explore effects of varied conditions and test hypotheses.

Chemical reactions are time dependent: the concentrations of reactants and products change continuously as the reaction occurs. Therefore, mathematical models treat concentration as a time dependent variable,while time acts as the independent variable. Instead of calculating one single value, this allows models to generate a sequence of values over time to calculate the concentration of each chemical species at different points in time. The results are plotted on a simple concentration vs time graph to visually describe the changes, allowing the reaction to be analysed throughout its duration instead of only at the initial and final states.

Changes in concentration are defined using differential equations which express the rate of change of concentration with respect to time. Differential rate of change is written in the form

d[A]/dt

where t represents time and [A] represents the concentration of species A. In these models, every chemical species is represented by a variable corresponding to its concentration e.g. the concentrations of substance A might be written as [A] and the concentration of substance B as [B] etc. This expression describes how rapidly the concentration is changing. A positive value would indicate an increasing concentration; whereas a negative value would indicate that it’s decreasing.

Numerical Methods

Differential equations are used to demonstrate how the concentration varies and often cannot be solved exactly to give an answer; instead they describe how the concentration changes at any given moment. Therefore, numerical methods are used to calculate concentration values at small time intervals, allowing the behaviour of the system to be simulated over time.

The Euler method works by dividing time into small and equal steps denoted by Δt. Using the differential equation, the rate of change of concentration is calculated at each time step. The value obtained is then used to estimate the concentration at the next time step. The Euler method approximates the concentration values by calculating them at discrete time steps rather than producing a continuous equation.

The Euler method is written in the form

[A]new = [A]initial + (d[A]/dt) × Δt

where [A] is the concentration of species A. In this equation, the new concentration is found by adding the change in concentration to the initial value. Repeating this many times allows for the concentration of each species to be monitored throughout the reaction.

The magnitude of the time step used can affect the accuracy: smaller time steps tend to produce more accurate results as larger steps result in greater rounding errors (Steiner, 2008). Despite this, the Euler method allows for an effective way to numerically model reaction systems and observe how the concentration of different species varies over time.
